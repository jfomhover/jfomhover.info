Piracy is usually treated as capitalism's outside: theft, disorder, or an attack on legitimate exchange. Rodolphe Durand and Jean-Philippe Vergne [[Durand, 2012](#durand2012), [Durand, 2013](#durand2013)] place it at the edge of capitalism's expansion, in partially charted territories where the rules of access, ownership, and exchange are still being worked out.

Their question is what happens when an existing boundary is unsettled.

## The Core Idea

For Durand and Vergne, piracy is an organizational form, not just a moral or legal label. Pirate organizations work at the edges of territories where authority, ownership, access, and legitimate exchange are still being defined. The territory may be geographic, technological, or biological. The common feature is a gray area between a new field of capitalist expansion and the norms being imposed on it.

Pirate organizations remain part of capitalism's story. States and legitimate firms may suppress their challenges to sovereign norms, property rules, and legitimate exchange, or absorb and reorganize what those challenges introduce. The recurring relationship is among states, firms, and organizations that contest the rules taking shape around them.

## The Mechanism

The pattern has three connected elements:

1. **A new territory enters capitalist expansion.** It is only partly charted, and its rules of ownership, access, and exchange remain unsettled.
2. **Normalization and piracy develop together.** Sovereign states map the territory and impose norms. Pirate organizations intervene while those first representations and rules are being established, challenging the order from its frontier.
3. **The struggle changes the order.** Legitimate firms profit within the normalized milieu, while pirate organizations introduce competing ideas about value, property, and distribution. States and firms may later suppress, absorb, or reuse those ideas.

Discovery is followed swiftly by normalization, but piracy appears during that early construction, before the territory's rules have settled. The struggle can shape the industries and business models that grow there.

## Historical Cases

Durand and Vergne compare several kinds of territory:

- **Maritime piracy**, contesting sovereign control over routes and trade.
- **Pirate radio**, challenging state control of the broadcast spectrum.
- **Cyberpiracy**, testing boundaries around copying, access, and digital distribution.
- **Biopiracy**, contesting ownership and commercial control over living material and biological knowledge.

The cases differ substantially. Their value is not that they are identical, but that each raises questions about authority, access, ownership, and legitimacy in a space whose rules are being established.

The comparison concerns a shared relationship to authority and boundary-making. It does not make every boundary-crossing actor a pirate organization.

## What It Says About Capitalism

Durand and Vergne emphasize that pirate and legitimate organizations confront one another within the same expansionary process. Legitimate firms normalize trade through recognized property rights; pirate organizations enact competing principles of value creation, value capture, and distribution in the gray areas at capitalism's frontier.[[Durand, 2012](#durand2012)]

Their framework complicates the opposition between legal capitalism and illegal piracy. A contested practice may be suppressed, absorbed, or reorganized into a new regime of value. It also raises a practical question: who benefits from the challenge, and who gets to define the settlement that follows?

The framework identifies a problem of incorporation without determining the social value of the resulting settlement.

## Critical Qualifications

Different cases may share a boundary problem without sharing the same organization, motive, or political consequences. Dumez's review is a useful warning against letting a broad comparison do more work than the evidence allows.[[Dumez, 2012](#dumez2012)]

Piracy is not automatically emancipatory. Some actors simply exploit others, and some settlements strengthen existing power or weaken public protection.

## Using the Framework

For a new technology or market, ask:

- What territory or mode of exchange is newly open to contestation?
- Which authority claims the right to define its boundaries?
- What do actors at the edge do that the existing rules cannot yet classify?
- Who captures value while the rules are unsettled?
- Does the system suppress the challenge, absorb it, or reorganize around it?
- What new boundary is created after the conflict?

These questions apply to platforms, data, intellectual property, infrastructure, and artificial intelligence. They leave the answer open: a disruptive actor may be a pirate organization, a criminal enterprise, a useful innovator, or some combination of these.
